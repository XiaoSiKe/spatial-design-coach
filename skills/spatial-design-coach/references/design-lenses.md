# Design Lenses

Read this reference only when a live design decision benefits from theory or a productive counterpoint. Select at most 2–3 lenses: usually one that sharpens the proposal and one that exposes its blind spot. Translate the questions into the student's site and artifact; never use a name as proof or imitate a master's visual signature.

The sources orient further reading. Verify editions, page references, translations, and quotations before academic citation.

## Select by the live conflict

Do not scan for a famous name. Start with the dominant conflict and choose a small tension set:

| Live conflict | Start with | Counter with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Universal order versus situated life | Vitruvius, Kahn, Alexander | Jacobs, Frampton, Wu Liangyong |
| Object versus relation or process | Rossi, Venturi/Scott Brown | Corner, Spirn, Lyle |
| New construction versus transformation | Kahn, Rossi | Lacaton/Vassal, Frampton |
| Expert optimization versus participation and justice | McHarg, Forman | Healey, Fainstein, Jacobs |
| Image or concept versus bodily use | Lynch, Corner | Pallasmaa, Gehl, Spirn |
| Fixed masterplan versus adaptation | Rossi, McHarg | Jacobs, Lyle, Corner |
| Local move versus territorial system | Kahn, Alexander | Geddes, Wu Liangyong, Yu Kongjian |

Choose only lenses whose questions can change a drawing, comparison, or verification. A new lens belongs here only after an acceptance case shows that the current set cannot expose a recurring design failure.

## Shared ways of thinking

### 1. Vitruvius — balanced obligations

- **Claim:** A work must hold technical soundness, usefulness, and experiential or formal quality together; excellence in one cannot excuse collapse in another.
- **Question:** Which obligation is currently carrying the project, and which one is being asked to disappear?
- **Use when:** A seductive form, efficient plan, or technical solution is being treated as sufficient by itself.
- **Blind spot:** The triad does not reveal whose utility or beauty counts, nor how ecological and political consequences are distributed.
- **Counterpoint:** Fainstein on justice; Rittel and Webber on contested public problems.
- **Source:** Vitruvius, *Ten Books on Architecture*, Book I.

### 2. Donald Schön — reflection in action

- **Claim:** Designing is a reflective conversation in which drawings and models talk back, reframing the problem through each move.
- **Question:** What did the last drawing reveal that your original problem statement could not?
- **Use when:** A student waits for a perfect concept before making, or repeats moves without learning from their consequences.
- **Blind spot:** Studio reflection can remain tacit and personal unless tested against evidence, affected people, and professional obligations.
- **Counterpoint:** McHarg on explicit evidence; Fainstein on public consequences.
- **Source:** Donald A. Schön, *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) and *Educating the Reflective Practitioner* (1987).

### 3. Bryan Lawson and Nigel Cross — designerly inquiry

- **Claim:** Problems and solutions co-evolve through iterative framing, conjecture, representation, and evaluation rather than linear deduction.
- **Question:** Which proposal has taught you something new about the problem, and how should the brief now be reframed?
- **Use when:** The student treats analysis, concept, and design as separate one-way phases.
- **Blind spot:** Designer-centered expertise can underplay participation, power, maintenance, and knowledge held outside the studio.
- **Counterpoint:** Healey on collaborative planning; Spirn on reading lived urban nature.
- **Source:** Bryan Lawson, *How Designers Think* (1980); Nigel Cross, *Designerly Ways of Knowing* (2006).

### 4. Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber — wicked problems

- **Claim:** Public design problems are contested, interconnected, and changed by attempts to solve them; there is no neutral final problem definition.
- **Question:** Who frames the problem differently, and which consequence makes your preferred framing contestable?
- **Use when:** A complex social or urban issue is reduced to a neat formal answer or a single metric.
- **Blind spot:** Calling everything wicked can become an excuse for indecision or weak accountability.
- **Counterpoint:** Lyle on operational systems; rescue-mode commitment to bounded, reversible decisions.
- **Source:** Horst W. J. Rittel and Melvin M. Webber, “Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning” (1973).

## Architecture

### 5. Louis Kahn — institution and served space

- **Claim:** Begin with what an institution and its rooms seek to enable, then give primary spaces and supporting systems a legible order.
- **Question:** What does this place want to let people do together, and which rooms or services make that possible?
- **Use when:** Program has become a list of areas with no hierarchy, character, or durable institutional idea.
- **Blind spot:** Essentializing what an institution “wants” can mute changing users, informal appropriation, and contested access.
- **Counterpoint:** Venturi and Scott Brown on ordinary use and contradiction; Lacaton and Vassal on freedom of use.
- **Source:** Louis I. Kahn, “Form and Design” (1961).

### 6. Christopher Alexander — fit and pattern relationships

- **Claim:** Recurring relationships among people, activities, and space can guide coherent form when adapted to a concrete context.
- **Question:** Which recurring conflict does this spatial pattern resolve, and what local condition requires it to change?
- **Use when:** Many local decisions lack a shared relational grammar or human-scale test.
- **Blind spot:** Patterns can appear universal, hide cultural conflict, or freeze behavior into an idealized norm.
- **Counterpoint:** Rossi on historical specificity; Rittel and Webber on irreducible disagreement.
- **Source:** Christopher Alexander, *Notes on the Synthesis of Form* (1964); Alexander et al., *A Pattern Language* (1977).

### 7. Aldo Rossi — memory and urban artifacts

- **Claim:** Architecture participates in collective memory through persistent types, monuments, and urban structures that outlast individual functions.
- **Question:** What existing form or trace carries shared memory here, and how can the project transform rather than merely decorate it?
- **Use when:** A proposal ignores inherited urban structure, typology, or the long life of civic form.
- **Blind spot:** “Collective” memory may privilege dominant narratives and turn history into fixed symbolism.
- **Counterpoint:** Jacobs on everyday change; Fainstein on whose city and whose memory.
- **Source:** Aldo Rossi, *The Architecture of the City* (1966).

### 8. Robert Venturi and Denise Scott Brown — complexity and the ordinary

- **Claim:** Contradiction, communication, convention, and everyday urban evidence can produce richer architecture than enforced purity.
- **Question:** Which inconvenient use, sign, convention, or contradiction is the clean concept excluding?
- **Use when:** Formal consistency has become more important than lived complexity or legibility.
- **Blind spot:** Learning from commercial environments can slip into uncritical reproduction of consumption, signage, and existing inequality.
- **Counterpoint:** Frampton on critical resistance; Pallasmaa on embodied experience beyond signs.
- **Source:** Robert Venturi, *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* (1966); Venturi, Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour, *Learning from Las Vegas* (1972).

### 9. Juhani Pallasmaa — multisensory embodiment

- **Claim:** Architecture is understood through the whole body, memory, material, sound, touch, temperature, and peripheral perception, not vision alone.
- **Question:** What will a body hear, touch, smell, feel, and remember along this sequence?
- **Use when:** The project is driven by aerial form, diagram, or rendering while occupation remains abstract.
- **Blind spot:** Phenomenological richness does not by itself resolve access, justice, program, cost, or environmental performance.
- **Counterpoint:** Vitruvius on multiple obligations; Fainstein on distributive consequences.
- **Source:** Juhani Pallasmaa, *The Eyes of the Skin* (1996).

### 10. Kenneth Frampton — critical regionalism

- **Claim:** Topography, climate, tectonics, material, and local culture can critically mediate universal production rather than imitate either global imagery or vernacular style.
- **Question:** Which local force changes the construction and spatial experience, rather than merely supplying a visual motif?
- **Use when:** A project is placeless, or “regional character” has been reduced to decorative quotation.
- **Blind spot:** Claims of authentic region can essentialize culture and overlook migration, hybridity, labor, and contemporary systems.
- **Counterpoint:** Venturi and Scott Brown on mixed contemporary signs; Healey on relational place-making.
- **Source:** Kenneth Frampton, “Towards a Critical Regionalism” (1983).

### 11. Lacaton and Vassal — transformation and spatial generosity

- **Claim:** Preserve and transform before demolishing; add usable, adaptable space and climatic capacity so occupants gain freedom rather than displacement.
- **Question:** What can be retained, enlarged, or made freely usable before you replace it?
- **Use when:** Demolition is assumed, existing buildings are undervalued, or minimum-area efficiency suppresses future use.
- **Blind spot:** Retention is not automatically just or ecological when hazards, operating burdens, land conflict, or unrepairable fabric are ignored.
- **Counterpoint:** Lyle on full resource cycles; Fainstein on who remains and who benefits.
- **Source:** Frédéric Druot, Anne Lacaton, and Jean-Philippe Vassal, *Plus: Large-Scale Housing Development—An Exceptional Case* (2007).

## Urban planning and design

### 12. Patrick Geddes — survey before plan

- **Claim:** Regional life, work, and place should be understood together before intervention; planning grows from situated diagnosis.
- **Question:** Which relationship among environment, livelihood, and settlement would your plan sever or strengthen?
- **Use when:** A master plan arrives before regional, social, economic, or environmental conditions are understood.
- **Blind spot:** Survey can delay action, privilege expert categories, or overlook people absent from official evidence.
- **Counterpoint:** Schön on learning through intervention; Healey on knowledge made collaboratively.
- **Source:** Patrick Geddes, *Cities in Evolution* (1915).

### 13. Jane Jacobs — everyday urban order

- **Claim:** Mixed primary uses, short blocks, building-age diversity, density, and continuous street observation can support adaptable urban life.
- **Question:** Who uses this street at different times, and what spatial and economic conditions keep those uses mutually present?
- **Use when:** Superblocks, separation, clearance, or iconic form erase ordinary networks and temporal diversity.
- **Blind spot:** Local vitality alone cannot supply regional infrastructure, affordability, redistribution, or protection from displacement.
- **Counterpoint:** Wu Liangyong on coordinated systems; Fainstein on justice beyond vitality.
- **Source:** Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961).

### 14. Kevin Lynch — legibility and environmental image

- **Claim:** Paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks shape how people recognize and navigate a city.
- **Question:** What mental map will different users form, and where does clarity need to give way to discovery or ambiguity?
- **Use when:** Movement, orientation, identity, or the hierarchy of public space is confused.
- **Blind spot:** Visual imageability can overlook nonvisual navigation, unequal access, fear, memory, and contested meanings.
- **Counterpoint:** Pallasmaa on multisensory experience; Fainstein on differentiated users.
- **Source:** Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City* (1960).

### 15. Jan Gehl — life between buildings

- **Claim:** Observe public life at walking speed and shape edges, dimensions, comfort, and invitations before composing distant form.
- **Question:** What optional or social activity becomes possible here at eye level, in real weather, at different times?
- **Use when:** Public space is impressive in plan but empty, oversized, uncomfortable, or weak at its edges.
- **Blind spot:** Fine-grained public-life design cannot substitute for housing policy, labor, governance, or regional mobility.
- **Counterpoint:** Healey on institutions; Wu Liangyong on larger settlement systems.
- **Source:** Jan Gehl, *Life Between Buildings* (1971) and *Cities for People* (2010).

### 16. Patsy Healey — collaborative planning

- **Claim:** Places are relational and planning knowledge is built through institutions, stakeholders, negotiation, and shared capacity rather than expert command alone.
- **Question:** Who must shape and carry this decision, and what process changes their actual influence rather than merely collecting comments?
- **Use when:** A proposal depends on cooperation, governance, or contested knowledge across groups.
- **Blind spot:** Consensus processes can conceal power, fatigue participants, or legitimate outcomes that remain unjust.
- **Counterpoint:** Fainstein on substantive justice; Rittel and Webber on persistent disagreement.
- **Source:** Patsy Healey, *Collaborative Planning* (1997).

### 17. Susan Fainstein — the just city

- **Claim:** Urban decisions should be judged by substantive equity, democracy, and diversity, not growth, process, or visual quality alone.
- **Question:** Who receives benefits, who bears costs or displacement, and what enforceable choice changes that distribution?
- **Use when:** Public value is asserted without identifying winners, losers, access, affordability, or voice.
- **Blind spot:** General justice criteria still require contextual judgment and may conflict with one another in implementation.
- **Counterpoint:** Healey on process and situated negotiation; Jacobs on emergent everyday order.
- **Source:** Susan S. Fainstein, *The Just City* (2010).

### 18. Wu Liangyong — sciences of human settlements

- **Claim:** Settlement problems require coordinated thinking across region, city, architecture, landscape, society, culture, technology, and time.
- **Question:** Which larger settlement system produces this local problem, and which local move can connect back without pretending to control the whole system?
- **Use when:** Architecture, planning, and landscape are being solved as isolated objects or at only one scale.
- **Blind spot:** Comprehensive coordination can become expert-led totalization unless local conflict, uncertainty, and bottom-up agency stay visible.
- **Counterpoint:** Jacobs on situated everyday intelligence; Rittel and Webber on limits to comprehensive resolution.
- **Source:** 吴良镛，《人居环境科学导论》(2001).

## Landscape and ecology

### 19. Ian McHarg — ecological suitability

- **Claim:** Layered environmental conditions can reveal different degrees of suitability and guide development away from avoidable ecological harm.
- **Question:** Which ecological process should determine where or how intervention occurs, and what evidence establishes that priority?
- **Use when:** Site allocation, infrastructure, or landform decisions ignore water, soil, habitat, hazard, or carrying capacity.
- **Blind spot:** Overlay methods can imply neutral expert optimization while hiding values, dynamic change, data uncertainty, and lived culture.
- **Counterpoint:** Corner on operative mapping; Fainstein on distributive choices.
- **Source:** Ian L. McHarg, *Design with Nature* (1969).

### 20. Anne Whiston Spirn — the city as nature

- **Claim:** Cities are part of nature; geology, water, climate, plants, infrastructure, language, and human life form one legible urban landscape.
- **Question:** Which natural process is already shaping daily urban life, and how can design make it safer, more legible, and more inhabitable?
- **Use when:** Ecology is treated as scenery outside the city or infrastructure is hidden from public understanding.
- **Blind spot:** Revealing process does not alone resolve ownership, maintenance, funding, or competing interpretations of place.
- **Counterpoint:** Healey on institutions; Lyle on operational resource cycles.
- **Source:** Anne Whiston Spirn, *The Granite Garden* (1984) and *The Language of Landscape* (1998).

### 21. Richard Forman — landscape mosaics

- **Claim:** Patches, corridors, matrices, edges, and flows explain how spatial configuration affects ecological continuity across scales.
- **Question:** Which patch, corridor, edge, or matrix relationship controls the flow you need to protect or redirect?
- **Use when:** Habitat, green infrastructure, regional connectivity, or fragmentation must inform spatial structure.
- **Blind spot:** Abstract ecological pattern can erase lived meaning, species-specific behavior, governance, and processes that do not map cleanly.
- **Counterpoint:** Spirn on cultural and experiential landscape; Corner on mapping as selective agency.
- **Source:** Richard T. T. Forman, *Land Mosaics* (1995).

### 22. James Corner — landscape as process and operative mapping

- **Claim:** Landscape is an active medium of change, and mapping can construct new relationships and possibilities rather than merely describe existing facts.
- **Question:** What hidden relation or future process can this map make operable, and what has its selection left out?
- **Use when:** A static master plan or descriptive map cannot engage change, uncertainty, program, or emergent occupation.
- **Blind spot:** Open-ended process and evocative mapping can evade measurable performance, accountability, and maintenance commitments.
- **Counterpoint:** McHarg on explicit suitability evidence; Lyle on closed-loop performance.
- **Source:** James Corner, “The Agency of Mapping” in *Mappings*, edited by Denis Cosgrove (1999).

### 23. John Tillman Lyle — regenerative systems

- **Claim:** Design should renew the ecological and resource systems it participates in, using cyclical flows and feedback rather than one-way consumption.
- **Question:** Which water, energy, nutrient, material, or stewardship loop can the project close or regenerate over time?
- **Use when:** Sustainability is an added feature rather than an operating relationship with site systems.
- **Blind spot:** Integrated cycles can become technically complex, management-heavy, or falsely self-sufficient without institutional capacity.
- **Counterpoint:** Jacobs on adaptable everyday networks; Lacaton and Vassal on using existing resources before adding systems.
- **Source:** John Tillman Lyle, *Regenerative Design for Sustainable Development* (1994).

### 24. Yu Kongjian — ecological security patterns

- **Claim:** Identify and protect the minimum spatial patterns needed to sustain critical ecological processes before allocating intensive development.
- **Question:** What minimum land-and-water structure must remain continuous for the site's essential ecological process to survive?
- **Use when:** Flood resilience, biodiversity, regional open-space networks, or development boundaries need a defensible spatial baseline.
- **Blind spot:** Security patterns can become technocratic zoning if land rights, local practices, experience, data uncertainty, and implementation power are ignored.
- **Counterpoint:** Healey on collaborative legitimacy; Spirn on lived urban nature.
- **Source:** Kongjian Yu, “Security Patterns and Surface Model in Landscape Ecological Planning,” *Landscape and Urban Planning* 36 (1996).
