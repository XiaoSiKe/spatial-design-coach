# Design Reasoning

Read this reference when forming a proposition, linking analysis to design, generating real alternatives, comparing them, or translating an idea into spatial mechanisms.

## Diagnose before generating

Locate the primary bottleneck:

- **Framing:** the student is solving the assigned object but not the situated problem.
- **Proposition:** the concept is a theme, virtue, metaphor, or desired mood without a mechanism.
- **Evidence:** analysis accumulates facts but does not change a decision.
- **Alternatives:** options are one scheme with cosmetic variation.
- **Spatialization:** the explanation is stronger than the plan, section, sequence, or system.
- **Integration:** local moves do not connect across scale, discipline, or time.
- **Evaluation:** the student cannot state why one consequence matters more than another.
- **Communication:** the project exists, but the submitted evidence cannot make it legible.

Do not solve every category at once. Select the one whose repair unlocks the others.

## Teach design as a learnable loop

When the student treats design as innate taste or a flash of inspiration, explain it through one concrete project move:

```text
notice a situated condition → frame what matters → change a spatial relation
→ represent the consequence → compare against a criterion → revise
```

Use plain language first, then introduce the precise term. Work from one relation to a system and from one drawing to cross-scale consistency. Do not front-load theory or a complete solution before the student has made and interpreted a move of their own.

## Write a testable proposition

Use this as a thinking scaffold, not mandatory final copy:

> For **[affected people or system]**, transform **[specific existing condition]** through **[spatial mechanism]**, so that **[observable spatial, social, or environmental consequence]**, while accepting or managing **[trade-off]**.

A sound proposition is:

- situated: it depends on this place, people, and brief;
- spatial: it changes relationships, dimensions, sequence, access, ground, form, or time;
- generative: it can guide more than one drawing or detail;
- contestable: a reasonable critic could prefer another value or mechanism;
- testable: an artifact or observation can support or weaken it;
- bounded: it does not promise to solve conditions beyond the project's reach.

Ask the student to restate it without design jargon. If the plain-language version collapses, the proposition is not ready.

For a philosophical proposition, use [design-lenses.md](design-lenses.md) to distinguish a source idea from the student's value choice and the coach's adaptation. A value such as belonging or restraint can guide what to test; it cannot establish a site fact or a performance result. Compare competing values through their spatial consequences under the same brief and evidence standard.

## Build an evidence chain

For every retained analysis item, complete this chain:

```text
observed or sourced condition
→ interpretation
→ design consequence
→ proposed move
→ representation or test
→ result and revision
```

Label the first link as supplied fact, direct observation, external source, inference, or assumption. A heat map, precedent image, quotation, or site photo is not a conclusion by itself.

Use precedents as transfer cases:

```text
problem → mechanism → spatial result → cost or blind spot → transfer conditions
```

Do not copy appearance before checking whether the mechanism, climate, users, governance, economics, and scale transfer.

### Audit existing datasets before adding more

When the student already has census, POI, mobility, remote-sensing, survey, or sensor material, do not automatically route new analysis or ask for more data. For each retained finding, check:

- **vintage:** whether sources describe compatible time periods;
- **granularity:** whether the spatial and temporal resolution can support the proposed decision;
- **coverage:** which places, people, periods, or behaviors are missing;
- **bias:** what collection method or platform overrepresents;
- **causality:** whether a correlation is being used as an explanation without evidence.

Choose one live decision and complete one full evidence chain before making another map. A heat or density pattern cannot become a node, axis, program, or demolition decision until the affected group, spatial consequence, scale, and falsifying evidence are named.

## Generate genuinely different alternatives

Hold the brief and essential constraints constant. Vary two or more consequential drivers:

- organizing logic: field, spine, cluster, courtyard, network, gradient, frame, or dispersed nodes;
- relationship to ground: preserve, carve, terrace, bridge, lift, flood, restore, or incrementally occupy;
- access and publicness: concentrated, distributed, layered, time-shared, controlled, or porous;
- program relation: separation, overlap, adjacency, shared threshold, productive conflict, or flexible reserve;
- environmental process: retain, slow, store, infiltrate, shade, ventilate, connect habitat, or allow succession;
- time and delivery: finished object, adaptable frame, phases, temporary-to-permanent, or stewardship-led change;
- value priority: equity, ecological continuity, intensity, memory, economy, freedom of use, or sensory experience.

Name each family by its mechanism, not a mood. Show all families with the same scale, north, base information, program assumptions, and evaluation questions.

Reject false alternatives when their circulation, section, public-private relation, environmental behavior, and implementation logic remain substantially identical. “Same person, three outfits” is a diagnosis; the repair is to change the skeleton.

## Compare without hiding judgment

First apply must-pass constraints: brief compliance, access and life safety requiring verification, site limits, minimum program, and deliverable feasibility. Do not let a weighted total compensate for a fatal failure.

Then compare consequences using 4–7 project-specific criteria. For each criterion record:

| Criterion | Evidence | Alternative A | Alternative B | Alternative C | Uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What matters here | How it will be tested | Consequence and trade-off | Consequence and trade-off | Consequence and trade-off | What could reverse the judgment |

Weights may clarify priorities but do not manufacture objectivity. If a small weight change reverses the winner, expose that sensitivity and make the value choice explicit.

A hybrid is valid only when mechanisms remain compatible. Do not collect every attractive feature into a scheme with no governing logic.

## Translate thought into space

For each pivotal proposition, test at least these translations when relevant:

- **Relation:** what becomes adjacent, separated, visible, shared, or inaccessible?
- **Dimension:** what width, depth, height, density, gradient, or capacity makes the relation real?
- **Section:** how do body, terrain, water, climate, structure, and program meet vertically?
- **Sequence:** what changes across approach, threshold, occupation, pause, and exit?
- **System:** how do circulation, ecology, structure, service, and governance reinforce or contradict one another?
- **Time:** what happens by hour, season, growth, disturbance, phase, maintenance, and adaptation?
- **Edge:** how does the project negotiate what lies beyond its formal boundary?

Select the cheapest representation that can falsify the move:

- relationship or hierarchy → diagram;
- access, adjacency, capacity, or boundary → plan;
- terrain, light, climate, structure, or vertical publicness → section;
- bodily experience and thresholds → sequence or physical/digital model;
- environmental or operational performance → calculation, simulation, scenario, or specialist check;
- change over time → phased plan, seasonal set, timeline, or stewardship protocol.

Do not ask an atmospheric rendering to prove access, dimensions, ecology, or feasibility. An image can testify to experience; it is not an alibi for unresolved space.

## Escalate help without taking over

Use the lightest intervention that can restart the student's reasoning:

1. **Question:** identify the contradiction the student should resolve.
2. **Hint:** narrow attention to one relation, section, actor, or consequence.
3. **Comparison:** place two mechanisms against the same criterion.
4. **Bounded example:** demonstrate one fragment, then ask the student to apply the principle elsewhere.
5. **Rescue path:** provide an ordered completion route while leaving pivotal choices explicit.

After a pivotal decision, ask for a short student restatement:

- What are you changing?
- Through what spatial mechanism?
- For whom or what system?
- What evidence supports it?
- What trade-off are you accepting?

### Use AI as a sparring partner

Before generating or routing alternatives, record the student's current intention and locked constraints. Then use AI to create a counterproposal, expose a contradiction, or vary a consequential mechanism—not merely multiply attractive outputs.

After comparison, ask the student to state:

- what they accept and why;
- what they reject and why;
- what changed in their own proposition;
- which new uncertainty must be tested.

Write those choices into the shared decision record. Do not treat prompt fluency or passive selection from generated images as design learning.

## Recognize common design failures

| Symptom | Real diagnosis | Useful repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “My concept is connection” | Desired virtue without an affected relation | Name what is disconnected, for whom, and the spatial mechanism |
| Many analysis diagrams | No evidence-to-decision chain | Keep only findings that change a move |
| Three similar schemes | Styling substituted for alternatives | Change organizing logic and value priority |
| Master-inspired form | Visual signature borrowed without conditions | Use the master's question and an opposing lens |
| Strong perspective, weak plan | Atmosphere masking unresolved organization | Test circulation, dimension, edge, and section |
| Good object, weak context | Project boundary treated as the world | Trace flows and consequences beyond the site |
| Everything is a priority | No design judgment | Name the dominant value and accepted loss |
| Great idea, unfinished set | Reasoning detached from submission | Enter delivery triage and update shared source drawings |

Design quality is not novelty alone. Look for coherence between claim and consequence, specificity to conditions, spatial richness, cross-scale continuity, experiential care, evidence, feasibility proportional to studio level, and an honest account of trade-offs.
